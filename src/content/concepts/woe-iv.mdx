## Weight of evidence

Does this bin hold more good customers or more bad customers than you'd expect?

<WoEBuilder client:visible />

<Misconception>

WoE is not the default rate. It compares who is in the bin, not how many in the bin default.

</Misconception>

<MathDetails summary="The math: WoE">

$$
\mathrm{WoE}_i = \ln\frac{\%\mathrm{good}_i}{\%\mathrm{bad}_i}
$$

- $\%\mathrm{good}_i$ is the teal-grey bar: the share of all good customers who sit in bin $i$.
- $\%\mathrm{bad}_i$ is the coral-grey bar: the share of all bad customers in bin $i$.
- The ratio is step 1, the $\ln$ is step 2, and the result is the big number in step 3.
- Positive means safer than the portfolio average, negative means riskier, and 0 means no information.

Some textbooks use $\ln(\%\mathrm{bad}_i / \%\mathrm{good}_i)$ instead, which flips every sign. This page always uses good over bad.

</MathDetails>

## Information value

WoE tells you how **one bin** leans. But a feature has several bins, and you want one answer: **how useful is this feature overall at separating good customers from bad ones?** That number is the **information value (IV)**. Think of it as a usefulness score for the feature.

A bin should count for more when two things are true:

1. **It leans clearly.** Its WoE is far from 0, so it is clearly safer or clearly riskier.
2. **It holds a lot of people who are sorted.** There is a big **gap** between its share of good customers and its share of bad ones. A bin with 2% of everyone cannot teach you much, however strongly it leans.

So each bin **contributes** its gap multiplied by its lean. Add up the contributions of all bins and you have the feature's IV.

<Callout title="A tiny example">

Bin A holds 40% of the good customers but only 10% of the bad ones. The gap is 30 points and it leans strongly safer (ln 4 = +1.39), so it contributes 0.30 × 1.39 = **0.42**.

Bin B holds 20% of the goods and 20% of the bads. There is no gap and it leans nowhere, so it contributes **0**: it tells you nothing.

A bin that leans riskier (negative lean) has a negative gap too, and a negative times a negative is positive. So **every bin contributes zero or more**: a risky bin is just as informative as a safe one.

</Callout>

<FeatureRanking client:visible />

**How to read it.**

- **Top chart:** one bar for one feature, cut into pieces. Each piece is one bin's contribution, and the whole bar is the feature's IV. Under it, each bin shows its gap × lean = contribution, so you can check the sum yourself.
- **Bottom chart:** every feature's IV on one scale. A longer bar means a more useful feature. The shaded bands are the conventional strength labels from the table below.

**Try this**

- Switch features in the top chart. Which bins make up most of the bar? Are they the biggest bins, or the ones that lean hardest?
- Turn on **Add a feature that peeks at the future**. Why is its bar so far to the right?

<Explain level={3} title="What just happened">

A feature has a high IV when its bins split good and bad customers into **very different groups**: some bins full of goods, others full of bads. A feature whose bins all look like the overall portfolio has an IV near 0, and is no use for telling customers apart.

</Explain>

<RulesOfThumb level={3} title="How strong is an IV?">

| IV          | Reading                       | What people do with it      |
| ----------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| below 0.02  | Not useful                    | Drop it                     |
| 0.02 to 0.1 | Weak                          | Usually drop, or combine it |
| 0.1 to 0.3  | Medium                        | Keep                        |
| 0.3 to 0.5  | Strong                        | Keep                        |
| above 0.5   | Suspicious: check for leakage | Investigate before using    |

These cutoffs are a convention, not a law.

</RulesOfThumb>

<MathDetails summary="The math: IV">

$$
\mathrm{IV} = \sum_i \left(\%\mathrm{good}_i - \%\mathrm{bad}_i\right)\,\mathrm{WoE}_i
$$

- $\%\mathrm{good}_i - \%\mathrm{bad}_i$ is the **gap** in the widget: how different the bin's share of goods and share of bads are. A bin where they match adds nothing.
- $\mathrm{WoE}_i$ is the **lean**: which way, and how strongly, the bin points.
- Their product is the bin's **contribution**. Both factors have the same sign, so it is never negative.
- The sum over all bins is the whole stacked bar: the feature's IV.

</MathDetails>

## Binning

You choose the bins. Move the edges and watch the evidence change.

<BinEditor client:visible />

**Try this**

- Press **Add split** until you have 8 bins. Do the WoE bars zig-zag?
- Choose **Equal width** on monthly turnover. Which bins get too small to trust?

<Explain level={3} title="Rules for good bins">

- **Start with equal-count bins.** Every bin has plenty of people, so no bin is a fluke.
- **Smooth WoE is a feature.** Steady steps mean a real trend; a zig-zag is usually noise.
- **Tiny bins mislead.** With a handful of defaulters, one more or fewer swings the WoE a lot.

</Explain>

## The noise trap

More bins always raises IV, even for a feature that means nothing.

<NoiseTrap client:visible />

**Try this**

- Drag the slider to 30 bins. When does the random number cross the 0.02 line?
- Press **Resample data** a few times. The random line wobbles, the bureau line stays above it.

<Misconception>

Higher IV is not always better. Chasing IV by adding bins just fits noise.

</Misconception>

## In practice

<InTheWild>

- **Why scorecards use WoE.** Every feature becomes a straight line in log-odds, which is what logistic regression assumes. Missing values get their own bin, and outliers just fall into an end bin.
- **Zero-defaulter bins** give an infinite WoE. Smooth them (add a small constant) or merge them with a neighbour.

</InTheWild>

<RulesOfThumb level={3} title="What people actually do">

- **Keep bins big enough:** at least 5% of people, or about 30 to 50 defaulters per bin. At a 4% default rate that allows only a handful of bins.
- **Do not trust IV alone.** It ranks features but does not see interactions, and it depends on your binning.
- **Check IV on resampled or out-of-time data.** At a 2 to 5% default rate it is noisy on small samples.
- **Workflow:** screen by IV, then remove near-duplicates using the [correlation page](/concepts/correlation), then build the model.

</RulesOfThumb>
