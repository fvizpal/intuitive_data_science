## 1. Two crowds

<Explain level={3} title="The idea">
  A good score keeps two crowds apart. Drag them closer and they blur together.
</Explain>

## 2. KS, the biggest gap

<Explain level={3} title="The idea">
  The score is our measure of risk: the lower the score, the riskier the customer. So line
  customers up by score, lowest first. Then walk down the line and keep two counts: how
  many bads you have passed, and how many goods. A good score puts most bads near the
  front, so the bad count races ahead of the good count and the gap between them grows.
  Later the line is mostly goods, and the gap shrinks again. The biggest gap is **KS**.
</Explain>

<KSWalk client:visible />

Same walk, drawn as two curves. Slide the cutoff to move along them.

<KSGap client:visible />

<Misconception>
  KS looks at one cutoff, the best one. It does not tell you what happens at the others.
</Misconception>

<MathDetails summary="The math: KS">

$$
\mathrm{KS} = \max_{c}\;\bigl|\,F_{\text{bad}}(c) - F_{\text{good}}(c)\,\bigr|
$$

- $F_{\text{bad}}(c)$ is the share of bads with a score below $c$: the dashed curve.
- $F_{\text{good}}(c)$ is the same for goods: the solid curve.
- KS is the tallest vertical gap between the two curves, anywhere.

</MathDetails>

## 3. AUC, the chance a good beats a bad

<Explain level={3} title="The idea">
  Pick a random good and a random bad. How often does the good one score higher? That
  chance is **AUC**.
</Explain>

<PickAPair client:visible />

<MathDetails summary="The math: AUC">

$$
\mathrm{AUC} = P(\text{good scores higher}) + \tfrac12\,P(\text{tie})
$$

- It compares every good with every bad, so the default rate does not change it.

**Counting pairs.** With $n_g$ goods and $n_b$ bads, score $s$:

$$
U = \sum_{i=1}^{n_g}\sum_{j=1}^{n_b}\Bigl[\mathbf 1(s_i > s_j) + \tfrac12\,\mathbf 1(s_i = s_j)\Bigr]
\qquad
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{U}{n_g\,n_b}
$$

$U$ is the Mann-Whitney statistic: the number of (good, bad) pairs where the good wins, with ties counting half.

**From ranks, without comparing every pair.** Put all $n_g + n_b$ customers in one list, lowest score first, and give each a rank $1, 2, 3, \dots$ (tied scores share the average of their ranks). Let $R_g$ be the sum of the goods' ranks:

$$
U = R_g - \frac{n_g(n_g+1)}{2}
\qquad
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{R_g - n_g(n_g+1)/2}{n_g\,n_b}
$$

- $n_g(n_g+1)/2$ is the smallest $R_g$ could be: every good below every bad, ranks $1, 2, \dots, n_g$. Subtracting it counts only the bads each good has beaten.
- Sorting once is enough, so this takes $O(n \log n)$ instead of $n_g \times n_b$ comparisons.

**Worked example.** Goods $= 2, 4, 6$ and bads $= 1, 3, 5$. Sorted: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, so the goods have ranks 2, 4, 6 and $R_g = 12$.

$$
U = 12 - \frac{3\cdot 4}{2} = 6
\qquad
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{6}{3\cdot 3} = 0.67
$$

Check by pairs: 2 beats 1; 4 beats 1 and 3; 6 beats 1, 3 and 5. That is $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$ wins out of 9 pairs.

</MathDetails>

## 4. ROC and Gini

<Explain level={3} title="The idea">
  Slide the cutoff and plot goods rejected against bads rejected. A better score bows
  further up and left. The area under the curve is AUC.
</Explain>

<ROCBuilder client:visible />

<Explain level={3} title="Gini">
  Gini rescales AUC so a coin flip is 0 and perfect is 1.
</Explain>

<MathDetails summary="The math: Gini">

$$
\mathrm{Gini} = 2\,\mathrm{AUC} - 1
$$

- AUC runs from 0.5 (coin flip) to 1 (perfect). Gini stretches that to 0 and 1.
- Coin flip: 2 × 0.5 − 1 = 0. Perfect: 2 × 1 − 1 = 1. AUC 0.76 gives Gini 0.52.
- A score that ranks the wrong way round has AUC below 0.5, so a negative Gini.

</MathDetails>

## 5. Reality check

<Explain level={3} title="The idea">
  So far we measured one score on one set of customers. In real work you only ever see
  one sample, and a different sample would give different numbers. Here the real scorecard
  from the last page scores a pool of 20,000 new applicants it has never seen, about 4% of
  them bad. We draw 40 random samples from the pool and measure Gini and KS on each. How
  far apart do the 40 answers land?
</Explain>

<SampleWobble client:visible />

**How to read it.** Each blue dot is one sample. The white diamond is the average of the
40. The black bar runs from the lowest to the highest result. The dashed coral line is
what the same scorecard scored on the customers it was built on.

**Try this**

- Set the sample size to 500, then draw new samples a few times. The bar gets wide, because
  500 customers hold only about 20 bads.
- Move to 5,000. The dots bunch up, because 200 bads give a steadier answer.
- Compare the dashed line with the diamond. Which one is higher?

<Explain level={3} title="What just happened">
  What matters is the number of bads, not the number of customers. With 20 bads, a few
  unlucky ones move Gini a lot, so one sample can say 0.3 and the next 0.6, from the same
  scorecard. Bigger samples wobble less. The dashed line sits to the right of the dots
  because a scorecard is fitted to its own data, so it looks best there. New customers
  rank a little worse. That is why practitioners test on fresh data and quote a range.
</Explain>

<Misconception>
  A high AUC does not mean the probabilities are right. A score can rank well and still
  give wrong PDs.
</Misconception>

## In practice

<InTheWild>

- KS, AUC and Gini measure ranking only, not whether predicted PDs are accurate.
- Compare train, test and out-of-time values; a big drop signals overfitting or drift.
- They ignore the default rate, but approval and bad-rate trade-offs depend on it.
- With few defaulters, report a range or confidence interval, not one number.
- Gini here is 2 × AUC − 1, not the Gini impurity used in decision trees.
- An unusually high KS or Gini deserves a leakage check before celebrating.

</InTheWild>

<RulesOfThumb level={3} title="How strong is the AUC?">

| AUC          | Reading                       |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- |
| below 0.55   | coin flip                     |
| 0.55 to 0.70 | weak                          |
| 0.70 to 0.80 | acceptable                    |
| 0.80 to 0.90 | strong                        |
| above 0.90   | suspicious: check for leakage |

These bands are conventions, not laws.

</RulesOfThumb>
