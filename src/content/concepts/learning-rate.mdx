**Try this**

- Find the one learning rate that lands in a single step.
- Find the smallest value that diverges.
- Drag the starting ball somewhere else. Do the same rates still behave the same way?

<Explain>

Each step moves by η × slope. Near the bottom the slope is small, so a small η crawls. A large η overshoots the minimum and lands where the slope is steeper on the other side.

</Explain>

<MathDetails summary="The math">

The update rule, one step of gradient descent:

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \nabla L(\theta_t)
$$

- $\theta_t$ is the ball's position (the dot you see).
- $\nabla L(\theta_t)$ is the slope under the ball. Steeper means a bigger step.
- $\eta$ is the learning-rate slider.

For this curve, $L(x) = (x-2)^2 + 1$, so the distance to the minimum shrinks by a fixed factor each step:

$$
x_{t+1} - 2 = (1 - 2\eta)\,(x_t - 2)
$$

The factor is positive below 0.5 (smooth), zero at 0.5 (one step), negative between 0.5 and 1 (bouncing), exactly −1 at 1 (stuck), and below −1 past 1 (diverging).

</MathDetails>

<Misconception>

A higher learning rate always trains faster. Past the stable range it never converges at all.

</Misconception>

<InTheWild>

In XGBoost, `eta` shrinks each tree's contribution. Lower `eta` (0.02–0.05) with more trees usually generalizes better on credit data than 0.3 with few trees, at the cost of training time.

</InTheWild>

<RulesOfThumb>

- If the loss goes up or turns to NaN, cut the learning rate by 3–10×.
- If the loss falls but crawls, raise it until it just starts to wobble, then back off.
- Pair a small learning rate with more steps (more trees in boosting), and use early stopping to decide when to quit.

</RulesOfThumb>
